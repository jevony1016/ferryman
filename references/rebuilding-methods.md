# Rebuilding methods (Step 3)

Purpose: from the confirmed facts, hard constraints, and the user's preferences, propose candidate options, then check and revise them.

Finding the principle is only half the job. "Now I understand the essence" with no new option is the stop-after-breaking-down trap.

## Hard vs soft constraints

- **Hard**: physical laws, mathematical relations, binding law or contract, health limits, the user's stated non-negotiables.
- **Soft**: habits, conventions, expectations, "how it's usually done".

Never mix them up. A soft constraint can be relaxed as an option; a hard one cannot. A removable constraint is a hypothesis to check, not permission to ignore contracts, safety, or other people.

---

## 1. Constraint back-solving

**Use when**: the core constraints are confirmed and you need a new design.

**Steps**
1. List all hard constraints, and the user's non-negotiable preferences.
2. Search inside the space they leave for the best option (best by the user's own goal).
3. Check every candidate against every constraint.

Often pairs with: repeated why (which surfaced the real constraints).

## 2. Extreme assumption

**Use when**: you found the key variable but don't know what changing it does.

**Steps**
1. Pick the variable with the largest effect on the outcome.
2. Push it to an extreme: zero, unlimited, doubled, removed entirely. ("If this cost nothing…", "If you had to decide today…", "If you could never change your mind…")
3. Watch how the situation changes; follow that direction back toward a realistic range.

The extreme is a tool, not the plan. Always return to realistic values.

Often pairs with: cost-component breakdown (which showed which variable is heavy).

## 3. Component recombination

**Use when**: every component looks fine, but the whole keeps failing.

**Steps**
1. List the components found while breaking down.
2. Rearrange from scratch: order, who does what, timing, grouping, what is done together or apart.
3. Check that the new structure uses only confirmed components, not smuggled-back assumptions.

Often pairs with: assumption audit (which showed which old structure was only convention).

## 4. Verification loop

**Use when**: an option has been built and you need to confirm it really follows from the base facts, not from after-the-fact justification.

**Steps**
1. Write the full chain: base facts → step → step → option.
2. At each step ask "what is this based on?"; mark anything resting on `consensus`, `authority`, or `guess`.
3. If there is a gap, go back and fix it; loop until no gap remains or the remaining gaps are explicit test targets.

Often pairs with: analogy removal (to check that copied structure did not sneak back in). Can be used after any method.

---

## Pairings are starting points, not rules

The pairings above are common, not fixed. In practice you move between three things: understand the problem, propose a change, check the result. Writing the process down shows where you are stuck and lets others point out what is missing.

## Bring experience back in

After rebuilding, revisit the conventions you set aside:

- **Keep** when their original conditions still hold.
- **Modify** when conditions changed.
- **Drop** when they survive only as habit, status, or imitation.

Experience, analogy, intuition, and decomposition are not rivals: experience proposes candidates, analogy offers leads, analysis confirms conditions and risks. You do not need to wait for the others to fail before checking their premises.
