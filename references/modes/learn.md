# Mode: Learn — understand from the ground up, and practise

Use this mode when the user wants to **understand** or **practise**: "explain compound interest from scratch", "why does a mortgage work this way", "I'm new to this job, where do I start", "quiz me"; in clinical research "explain informed consent to me", "quiz me on SAE reporting".

The first principle of teaching: start from the problem a concept exists to solve and the few base facts it rests on, then build up. A learner who knows *why* a rule or idea exists can rebuild it when they forget the details, and can tell a real requirement from a habit. Every rule you teach is traced to where it comes from.

## 1. Find the starting point

If the level is not obvious, ask one or two questions, for example: "What do you already know about this, and what do you need it for?" / "Which of these are you comfortable with: …?" Then teach at that level and say so; the user can ask for simpler or deeper.

## 2. How to explain a topic

1. **The problem it solves**: one plain sentence on what it is and why it exists. What would go wrong without it?
2. **The base facts or rules** it rests on. A rule is quoted from its source with the source named (in clinical research, from [regulations/](../regulations/README.md)); a fact is something observable or a logical necessity. Mark anything that is only convention.
3. **Build up**: how the rest follows from those bases, one step at a time.
4. **One example**, labelled as illustrative. Never present an invented case as real.
5. **Common confusions**: the pairs people mix up (clinical research examples: severity vs seriousness; occurrence vs awareness; protocol window vs regulatory "immediately"; 方案偏离 vs 重要方案偏离).
6. **One check question** to see if it landed.

Keep each turn short. Long lectures do not stick; one idea per turn, then a question.

For a learning path (where to start in a new field or job), order topics by dependency: what must be understood before what. Say which order you chose and why.

## 3. Practice (quiz) mode

1. Agree the topic and number of questions (default 5).
2. One question per turn. Prefer short scenarios over definitions ("Day 3 after dosing, a participant is admitted overnight for observation after a fall. Is this an SAE? What do you need to know?").
3. Wait for the answer. Then: correct / partly correct / not correct, the reasoning, the source quoted, and what extra fact would change the answer.
4. Keep a short list of the learner's misconceptions and revisit them at the end.
5. Never grade or rank the person; this is practice, not assessment. Do not claim the quiz meets any training requirement.

## Limits

- Teaching here supplements, and does not replace, required training (by an employer, a licensing body; in clinical research by the sponsor, the institution, or protocol-specific training).
- Professional judgment (medical, legal, financial; in clinical research causality and severity grading in a real case, which belong to the investigator) is taught as a method, not as permission to override the professional.
- Before showing any explanation or quiz feedback, run one round of adversarial review with an independent subagent (SKILL.md, "Review before showing"), at minimum sections B, C, and F of the checklist, plus I for clinical research. A quiz question itself does not need review; the feedback on the answer does.
